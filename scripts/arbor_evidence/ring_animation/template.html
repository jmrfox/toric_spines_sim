Color is depolarization from rest. The ring is 2 µm in radius (0.25 µm cable), with a 1.5 µm neck into a sink carrying the whole cell's 205 pF and 290 MΩ (Sanculi medians). The white tick at the top is the cut; the dot is the synapse (4 nS AMPA at t = 1 ms).

What to look for. With the loop closed, charge flows both ways around the ring, so the left side of the cut lights up as fast as the right. With the loop cut, the left side has to wait for charge to travel the long way round. At the default weight (1 µS) the junction looks seamless, with no visible step at the cut, but the whole spine responds at less than half the correct amplitude.

A weak junction acts like a thin resistor: the cut stays visible as a step. A strong junction removes the step but drains the response. Arbor's own example uses 0.015 µS; this repo used 1 µS.

Why strong junctions drain the response. Arbor computes the junction current from the peer's voltage at the start of the step and the local voltage at the end. Added over both ends, that current opposes any change in the pair's voltage, exactly like a capacitor of size g·dt on each side. At 1 µS and dt = 0.025 ms that is 50 pF placed inside a ring whose real membrane is about 0.2 pF; at 10 µS it is 500 pF, more than twice the whole neuron.

Checked directly: the correctly closed loop with an extra 2·g·dt capacitor at the cut reproduces Arbor's ring peak to within 0.02 mV at 1 and 10 µS, at both dt = 0.025 and 0.0125 ms. Halving dt halves the fake capacitance, so the result also depends on the time step, which a physical connection never does.

Why it never showed up in the animations

Two reasons it stays hidden. First, the visible error is a wrong amplitude, not a glitch: a smooth, plausible attenuation looks like physics. Second, the voltage across the junction zigzags up and down on alternate simulation steps, and recording every 0.25 ms (an even number of steps) lands on the same phase each time, so the zigzag disappears from the recording entirely.

The mechanism, with two compartments

Compartment A starts at 10 mV and B at 0 mV, joined by a strong conductance (k = g·dt/C = 100, a cable joint is ~10⁴). In the cable, Arbor solves both new voltages together. A gap junction instead uses the other side's voltage from the start of the step.

Each side jumps to where the other side was, so they swap places every step. Charge crosses one way, then straight back, and the net transfer shrinks as the conductance grows.
